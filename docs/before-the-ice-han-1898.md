# Hän families rebuild downriver as Dawson booms

*Research for Martha Paul in [Before the Ice Goes Out](before-the-ice-goes-out.md#the-people-downriver), November 2026.*

In April–May 1898 the Hän of the Klondike, the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, were a community of perhaps 70 to 200 people living next to a boomtown of tens of thousands. Within 18 months they had lost their summer fish camp, Tr'ochëk, at the mouth of the Klondike, and their salmon traps. The Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in's own account says that after an agreement made over the winter of 1897–98 between Chief Isaac, the Anglican church and government officials, people were building cabins at Moosehide, about three miles downriver, "by spring". That places a Hän character of the game's period in the middle of a move. Spring was not salmon season. It was when late-winter hunting ended, people fished for grayling, hunted returning waterfowl, beaver and muskrat, and repaired canoes, nets and drying racks before the late-June chinook run. The cash economy of the rush ran alongside that cycle: Hän hunters sold moose and caribou in a hungry town, and Hän women sold moccasins and gloves at prices that had risen almost tenfold. Their relations with outsiders were double-edged. The North-West Mounted Police (NWMP) and the Anglican mission claimed to protect them, while their own officers wrote of them with open contempt. Liquor law under the Indian Act allowed police to arrest a Hän person without a warrant. A Hän witness could legally give evidence on affirmation, with an interpreter, but **no source found shows how much a Hän witness's word actually counted in 1896–98**, and **no source ties any Dawson fire to Indigenous people**. Real people, Hän songs and ceremony, the Hän language and the modern Moosehide Gathering are not a non-Hän writer's to borrow. The guidance that exists, from the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in Heritage Act and imagineNATIVE's screen protocols, points to one step: go to the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in Heritage department first, and pay and credit their reviewers.

**How sources are marked.** **[TH]** means published by the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in themselves (trondek.ca, Heritage department documents, the Chief Isaac interpretive sheet, the Fortymile interpreters' manual, the Heritage Act, the nation's UNESCO support letter). **[Co-authored]** means outside publications built with or co-owned by First Nations including TH: the First Nations interpretive manual co-owned by four Yukon First Nations, which draws on *Hammerstones*, and the *Dictionary of Canadian Biography* entry on Isaac, co-written by a Parks Canada historian who worked with TH. **[Indigenous]** means other Indigenous-led sources (imagineNATIVE, the Yukon Native Language Centre working with Hän Elders). **[Outside]** means settler or outside sources: period journalists, missionaries and officials, historians, Wikipedia and game developers. Where a TH document reprints a settler voice, such as an 1898–1932 newspaper, that voice is marked as outside even though TH published it. Some claims come only from search-result summaries, not pages read in full, and they are flagged as such.

## April–May meant grayling, ducks and canoe repair, not salmon

The nation's own heritage manual gives the clearest account of the spring round. **[TH]** In late winter, "family groups would once again disperse as winter food stores were depleted," hunting lone animals. "In early spring, people would harvest freshwater fish, such as Arctic grayling, at nearby lakes and streams and would hunt small game," with "returning waterfowl, beaver, and muskrat" added to the diet. "By late spring, the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in were back along the Yukon River and preparing canoes, fish weirs, and nets for the fishing season" ([TH Best Practices for Heritage Resources, p. 7](https://www.trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/TH-Best-Practices-Heritage-Resources.pdf)). In late spring, small family groups joined into fish camps of **about 20–50 people** and spent the wait "rebuilding canoes, fish traps, nets, drying racks, and shelters, as well as tanning caribou and moose hides for clothing and trade." **The chinook ran in late June to early July and the chum in August to early September** (same source), so there was no salmon in April–May. **[Outside]** The National Park Service's summary of Osgood agrees: cached food usually ran out "generally in February", people then fished through the ice and hunted moose, caribou, sheep, rabbits and grouse, and in spring returned to river camps to make canoes and nets, hunting waterfowl in pairs. It records birchbark canoes of three kinds, snowshoes, and a ten-foot sled "pulled by women and dogs" ([NPS, *Yukon Frontiers*, ch. II](https://npshistory.com/publications/yuch/grauman/chap2.htm)).

Break-up itself has a specific meaning for Tr'ochëk. **[TH]** There, "ice gouges the banks and reshapes the landscape," and floods periodically wiped out camps, which people rebuilt on the new silt ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)). The nation's Fortymile interpreters' manual stresses how much the supply line depended on the river: the steamer *Arctic* was "wrecked: 1897 in an ice jam at Circle City during spring break-up" ([TH, Early Traders and Steamboats, p. 5](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Early_Traders.pdf)). **The research found no source giving the date of break-up at Dawson in 1898, and no Hän-specific account of the weeks when the ice made the river impassable**, so the game should not state a date as fact.

The 1897–98 cycle had already absorbed the rush. **[Outside, period witness]** Tappan Adney, in Dawson that winter, described the "Tro-chu-tin" winter hunt as one in which the whole village went out, and noted that hunters found it "more profitable to sell meat to the increasing number of white men." He estimated that "probably one hundred and fifty" moose were killed around Dawson in winter 1897–98 "by Indians and white men" ([Adney, *The Klondike Stampede*, pp. 443, 451–453](https://archive.org/stream/klondikestampede00adnerich/klondikestampede00adnerich_djvu.txt)). **[Co-authored]** In January 1898 Adney went with Isaac and several Hän families up the Klondike, and the party sent **about 80 moose and 65 caribou** to Dawson to sell ([DCB, "ISAAC"](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/isaac_16E.html)). **[TH]** Photographs show Isaac fishing for grayling through the ice near Dawson around 1900, and paddling a birchbark canoe near Dawson in 1898 with his son and his brother Walter Benjamin ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet, pp. 4–5](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)), although "pole boats replaced birchbark canoes soon after the gold seekers arrived" ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)). From these sources, a Hän adult in April 1898 would plausibly be finishing a late-winter hunt or a meat-selling trip. In May they would be fishing grayling, hunting ducks, geese and muskrat, mending canoes and nets, tanning and sewing, and building at Moosehide. That picture is inferred from the sources above.

## Tr'ochëk was lost within a month and buried under "Lousetown"

**[TH]** Tr'ochëk, the point of land at the Klondike mouth across that river from present-day Dawson, is "the heart of Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in traditional territory." It holds at least six layers of occupation, and fish traps stood there "way before the white people came to this country," in Elder Stanley Roberts's words. People wove branches on stakes across the river mouth to drive salmon into basket traps, and fished from canoes with dipnets of caribou babiche ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)). The nation's name is tied to this place: "Tr'o" refers to the **hammerstones used to pound the trap stakes into the riverbed** ([trondek.ca, Our Heritage](https://trondek.ca/?p=175)), and "Klondike" comes from the Hän *Tr'ondëk* ([FNIC manual, p. 130](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). Outsiders wrote it down as "Thron Diuck" (Ogilvie, 1895) or "Thron'duick", glossed "hammer water" ([Neufeld, *Legion Magazine*](https://legionmagazine.com/trampled-in-the-rush/)).

Gold was found on Bonanza Creek on **16 August 1896**. **[TH]** "Within a month," stampeders had forced the Hän off Tr'ochëk. "Over the next year, mining destroyed the fish traps and ended salmon fishing at the mouth of the Klondike River. The camp was buried under Lousetown" ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)). **[Co-authored]** The interpretive manual explains how it happened. Joe Ladue sold Dawson lots at $300, and many miners would not pay that much, so they squatted at Tr'ochëk instead, staking "the spaces between Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in dwellings." In 1896 the Hän "accepted anywhere between $50 and $200 for the dwellings… The miners assumed they had bought the land as well. Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in thought that they were only selling the buildings and that they would be moved to Dawson City" ([FNIC manual, pp. 130–131](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). That disputed sale, in which the buyers thought they had bought the land and the sellers thought they had sold only the buildings, is the part of the record most relevant to a story about a store, property and blame. **[Outside, period witness]** On 29 May 1897 the missionary Flewelling wrote that "five or six hundred men have come here this spring alone, and their tents are scattered everywhere" (quoted secondhand in [Wikipedia, Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in First Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%CA%BCond%C3%ABk_Hw%C3%ABch%CA%BCin_First_Nation)). The site later held tents, a sawmill, a brewery, saloons, a store and brothels ("cribs"), and was known as Klondike City or "Lousetown" ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)).

The Hän moved twice. **[TH]** They "first moved across the river to Dawson City, to the new Mounted Police reserve, but soon needed a base farther from newcomers" (same source). **[Co-authored]** The manual is blunter: "The police saw the First Nations people as undesirable neighbours, squatters on officially surveyed land" ([FNIC manual, p. 131](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). A Hän person in spring 1898 could look across the Klondike at saloons standing where their family had camped two summers earlier.

## Chief Isaac negotiated a move that the sources date differently

**[Co-authored]** Isaac was born around 1850, probably in Alaska. He married Eliza Harper around 1890, and succeeded her father Gäh St'ät ("Catsah"), a hereditary chief, sometime in the early 1890s. Bishop Bompas baptized him around 1892, "probably when he got his English name" ([DCB, "ISAAC"](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/isaac_16E.html)). **[TH]** Elders say he came from "the 'Alaska side'", with no agreement on exactly where. He and Eliza had 13 children, four of whom survived to adulthood. "No record has been found of his original name," and one reason he was appointed chief may have been "his command of English and ability to negotiate with the newcomers" ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet, p. 2](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)). The nation remembers him as walking "a middle way between the gospel and the traditions of his ancestors": he attended Anglican synods and also presided at potlatches.

**[Co-authored]** With the missionary Frederick Flewelling's help, Isaac wrote to Ottawa asking for a place to settle and for the salmon fishery to be protected for his people. In late 1896 William Ogilvie asked Canada to protect "the fishery at the very least." The mail took two months each way, and "a full year had passed by the time word was received." In the meantime the church, the NWMP and the Hän agreed on a relocation, and the fishery request "for all practical purposes" was ignored ([FNIC manual, p. 131](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). **[Outside]** Ottawa told Ogilvie that "the Indians were not to be recognized in any way which would lead them to believe" the government would act for them ([Neufeld](https://legionmagazine.com/trampled-in-the-rush/)). **[TH]** His daughter Patricia (Isaac) Lindgren gave the family's account of his motive in 1977: "He wanted his people to be moved away from the city… afraid that his people would learn bad habits from the white people, drinking and trouble like that… so he talked to government and got them moved three miles down to Moosehide" ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet, p. 3](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)).

**The date of the move conflicts across sources.** The nation's website says "over the winter of 1897-98, Chief Isaac, church officials, and government officials agreed the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in would move downriver to Moosehide. **By spring, people were building cabins there**" **[TH]** ([trondek.ca, Tr'ochëk](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)). The DCB dates the negotiation to winter 1896–97 and the resettlement to summer 1897 **[Co-authored]** ([DCB](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/isaac_16E.html)). Neufeld and Wikipedia say spring 1897, with the reserve surveyed or established only in 1900 (27 March 1900, per Wikipedia) **[Outside]** ([Neufeld](https://legionmagazine.com/trampled-in-the-rush/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%CA%BCond%C3%ABk_Hw%C3%ABch%CA%BCin_First_Nation)). A NiCHE blog says the community "moved in 1900," which probably confuses the move with the reserve date ([NiCHE](https://niche-canada.org/2025/08/21/the-ubc-digitization-centre-and-indigenous-stories-of-the-klondike-gold-rush-part-2/)). The most defensible reading treats the move as a process: talks from late 1896, some families camping at Moosehide from 1897, a formal agreement over the winter of 1897–98, cabins going up in spring 1898, and a reserve in 1900. This is an interpretation, not a documented sequence, but it places the game's April–May exactly where the nation's own website places cabin-building.

Moosehide was a traditional campsite just below Moosehide Creek, chosen for "fresh water, timber, trails to hunting grounds, and salmon fishing" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%CA%BCond%C3%ABk_Hw%C3%ABch%CA%BCin_First_Nation)). "Three miles" (Lindgren; DCB) and "5 km" ([FNIC manual](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)) agree, since three miles is about 4.8 km. **What physically stood at Moosehide in 1898 is undocumented** in the sources reached. **[Outside]** St. Barnabas Church was not built until 1908, and the oldest grave in the cemetery behind it dates from 1898 ([Wikipedia, Moosehide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moosehide)). One outside source reports that Flewelling bought a 40-acre tract downstream for a mission, but the acreage is unconfirmed (Wikipedia, as above). Isaac's best-known words, "All Klondike belong my people… White man kill all", date from **December 1911**, not 1898, and **no verbatim Isaac statement dated 1896–98 was found** ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet, p. 4](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)).

## Famine-priced meat and $7 moccasins brought Hän cash to traders' stores

Trading was nothing new to the Hän in 1898. **[Co-authored]** In 1847 an HBC trader called them "sharp traders" ([DCB](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/isaac_16E.html)). **[TH]** Fort Reliance (1874), the first post in their territory, was built "responding to a request from Catsah," Isaac's father-in-law. The Hän helped put up the building and kept it supplied with meat. The traders McQuesten, Mayo and Harper, all married to Koyukon women, were "true partners… who acted as links between First Nations people and the newcomers" ([TH, Early Traders, pp. 1–3](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Early_Traders.pdf)). **[Outside]** By trading custom, Native customers expected days of tea, tobacco and meals at the trader's expense before any bartering, and they played rival firms off against each other. Disputes over goods were settled by negotiated compensation, not by courts. After an arsenic-grease poisoning at Charley's Village, McQuesten "settled" the deaths by giving a dog, and the villagers paid for goods stolen earlier ([NPS, *Yukon Frontiers*, ch. IV](https://npshistory.com/publications/yuch/grauman/chap4.htm)). **[TH]** The nation's manual also refers to "the arsenic incident at Reliance" ([Early Traders, p. 3](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Early_Traders.pdf)). That a quarrel between Hän people and a store could be settled by compensation is directly relevant to a burned-store plot.

During the rush, the most valuable goods the Hän sold were meat and sewn clothing. **[Outside]** Meat from Isaac's 1897–98 hunt sold at about **$1.25–$1.50 a pound**, and his villagers spent the proceeds on clothing, repeating rifles and food at the Alaska Commercial Company store ([Gates, Yukon News, hunting](https://www.yukon-news.com/letters-opinions/when-hunting-wild-game-was-big-business-in-the-yukon/); [Gates, "Chief Isaac: the gentle diplomat"](https://www.yukon-news.com/letters-opinions/chief-isaac-the-gentle-diplomat/)). These figures trace back to Adney alone. **[Co-authored, citing *Hammerstones*]** "In December 1897, [Moosehide women] were earning $20 to $40 per day making moccasins and gloves for the miners. Within a few months, the price of native moosehide moccasins had escalated from **75 cents to seven dollars**. Moosehide gloves now cost **eight dollars**." Men worked for Dawson and Klondike City businesses and freighters, at the sawmill and at O'Brien's Brewery, and "hunters sold meat to the town in great quantities" ([FNIC manual, p. 132](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). **[Outside]** Bishop Bompas wrote in April 1897 that Indians were renting out dogs at a dollar a day ([Cody, *Apostle of the North*](https://anglicanhistory.org/canada/bompas/cody1908/16.html)). From 1898–99, white market hunters began to compete: about a dozen of them supplied roughly 1,000 caribou to Dawson in 1899 ([Gates](https://www.yukon-news.com/letters-opinions/when-hunting-wild-game-was-big-business-in-the-yukon/)). **No source reached describes store credit or debt extended to Hän customers in 1897–98.** The credit that is documented, grubstaking, went to miners, so a Hän character's account book at the burned store would be invented detail.

## Police, mission and stampeders combined protection with contempt

**[Outside]** The NWMP came to the Yukon in 1894–95 partly because Bompas pressed Ottawa about Indigenous welfare and the whisky trade ([Galt Museum](https://nwmp.galtmuseum.com/chapters/the-gold-rush-and-the-policing-of-the-yukon)). Yet in January 1896 Inspector Constantine called the local people "a lazy shiftless lot," accused them of living off miners through the prostitution of their women, and called Bompas "a disturbing element" ([Canadian Mysteries, Constantine letter](https://canadianmysteries.ca/sites/klondike/context/4186en.html)). His 1896 report shows a force that was short of food and preferred fines, deportation or reprimand to jail ([NWMP Report 1896](https://www.explorenorth.com/library/history/NWMP-1896.html)). By 1898 there were nearly 300 police in the Yukon, based at Fort Herchmer in Dawson, mainly handling claim disputes, cache thefts, assaults and liquor ([Galt Museum](https://nwmp.galtmuseum.com/chapters/the-gold-rush-and-the-policing-of-the-yukon)). Two outside historians disagree about which land the police wanted. Gray says Constantine rebuffed Isaac's request for land on the Dawson side, wanting it for his own compound, and suggested the families live on river islands, which Isaac refused ([Canada's History](https://www.canadashistory.ca/explore/peace-conflict/reconsidering-the-gold-rush)). Henderson says the police had their eye on the Moosehide site itself, and Isaac "stayed firm" ([NiCHE](https://niche-canada.org/2025/08/21/the-ubc-digitization-centre-and-indigenous-stories-of-the-klondike-gold-rush-part-2/)). Isaac later spoke up in Dawson for Hän people accused of petty crimes or drunkenness, and argued in the newspapers that justice fell harder on Indigenous offenders. Gray gives no dates for this, and it probably covers the years after 1898.

The Anglican mission was the Hän's loudest outside advocate. **[Outside]** Bompas, bishop of Selkirk from 1891 and based at Buxton Mission at Forty Mile, lobbied for Indigenous interests and alienated many white residents of Dawson ([DCB, Bompas](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/bompas_william_carpenter_13E.html)). He also held openly contemptuous views of the Hän, according to Wikipedia's summary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%CA%BCond%C3%ABk_Hw%C3%ABch%CA%BCin_First_Nation)). Flewelling served the Dawson area from 1896 to 1898, and **B. Totty succeeded him at Moosehide in 1898** ([Yukon Who Is Who](https://yukonwhoiswho.ca/doku.php?id=t%3Ab_totty)). Bompas himself lived at Moosehide only in 1899–1900, when Mrs. Bompas recorded him vaccinating people against smallpox over their protests ([Cody](https://anglicanhistory.org/canada/bompas/cody1908/16.html); [Bompas letters](https://anglicanhistory.org/women/bompas/10.html), search summary). Coates, as summarized by others, describes Catholic competition prompting a "rush for souls" in which baptism standards were lowered ([Canadian Mysteries](https://canadianmysteries.ca/sites/klondike/context/firstnationshistory/4381en.html)).

Stampeders took the land and the game. **[Co-authored]** "In most cases First Nations people welcomed the newcomers as they would any visitors… It was not until it became clear that their resources were being depleted at a rapid rate that people began to speak out." The losses were "traditional food resources, new diseases, and the introduction of alcohol," and wage work kept families near Dawson when they would otherwise have been out harvesting ([FNIC manual, pp. 131–132](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). **[Outside]** Gray adds that stampeders vandalized fishing gear and cut the forests for firewood. She also notes that Hän meat "kept many Dawson residents from starving" in 1897–98 ([Canada's History](https://www.canadashistory.ca/explore/peace-conflict/reconsidering-the-gold-rush)). White patients refused to share hospital wards with Native patients ([Canadian Mysteries](https://canadianmysteries.ca/sites/klondike/context/firstnationshistory/4381en.html)). **No documented epidemic among the Klondike Hän in 1897–98 was found**, and the game should not invent one.

## A Hän witness could testify on affirmation, but no record shows how much the word counted

The law can be documented, but how it worked in practice cannot. **[Outside, statute]** The 1876 Indian Act let a judge, magistrate or coroner take evidence from an Indian "destitute of the knowledge of God" on solemn affirmation instead of oath. In inquests and criminal trials the substance had to be written down, signed or marked, and verified by an interpreter. The witness was warned of the penalty for lying, and a false affirmation counted as perjury. In liquor cases Indians were expressly "competent witnesses" (ss. 74–79). Under s. 83, a constable could arrest an intoxicated Indian without a warrant, and could add up to 14 days' jail for **refusing to name the source of the liquor**. Supplying liquor to an Indian carried one to six months' jail and a $50–$300 fine ([Indian Act 1876](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1100100010252/1100100010254)). The text in force in 1898 was the Revised Statutes of 1886, c. 43, which the research could not open, so section numbers and penalties may differ. By inference, a baptized Hän witness could probably swear an ordinary oath, and an inspector holding the liquor-source clause had leverage over any Hän person found drunk.

There is no record of how this played out in Dawson. **No 1896–98 document was found in which a Yukon official credits or discounts a Hän witness**, and no court case naming a Hän defendant or witness was found. The closest case is the trial of the Nantuck brothers, who were Tagish, not Hän, for a May 1898 shooting. It was tried in Dawson through an interpreter, and the record reportedly shows deep cultural misunderstanding. Two of the brothers were hanged in August 1899 (search summary only: [Executed Today](https://www.executedtoday.com/?p=20655)). The *Yukon Sun* index notes that Dawson papers mocked Isaac's "broken English" (search summary only: [Yukon Sun index](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/Yukon_Sun_Index_Summary_1898_1905.pdf)). Credibility probably ran through intermediaries such as Isaac, the mission and the old-time traders. That makes a reasonable game mechanic, but it is an inference, not a finding.

Dawson's documented fires were blamed on the saloon world, and no source found connects any of them to Indigenous people. The fire of 25 November 1897 began when a dance-hall woman threw a lamp at a rival in the M & N Saloon. Sources disagree on whether she was "Dolly" or "Belle" Mitchell. The fire of 26 April 1899 started at the Bodega Saloon, and the damage grew because the fire engine's firebox had gone out ([Dawson history](https://walter-us.net/Alaska/main/Dawson_City/dawson_history.htm); [Yukon Nuggets](https://yukonnuggets.com/stories/dawson-city-fire-1899), search summaries). **A plot in which townspeople suspect a Hän person of arson would be the game's own invention.** It can draw on the documented prejudice described above, but it has no historical precedent in these sources.

## English baptismal names fit 1898, and Hän words need permission

**[TH]** Even Isaac's Hän name was never recorded ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)). The names that survive are English or biblical given names with English surnames, from baptism. The TH sheet names Isaac's wife Eliza Harper, the surviving children Patricia Lindgren, Angela Lopaschuck, Charlie and Fred Isaac, and his brothers Jonathan Wood and Walter Benjamin. A 1907 potlatch photo at Eagle lists Edward Wood, Paul Chancy, Ben and Esau Harper, Charlie Steve, Billy and Andrew Silas, David Taylor, David Roberts, Peter Thompson, Jonathan Johnson, "Canadian Joe" and "Chief Alex". That gathering was at Eagle, so not everyone in it was Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in (same source). Outsiders called the people "Klondike Indians" or "Tro-chu-tin" (Adney), and in 1911 a newspaper recorded Isaac calling them "Moosehide". **[TH]** The name *Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in*, roughly "the people who lived at the mouth of the Klondike," was reclaimed after the land-claim settlement ([trondek.ca, Our Heritage](https://trondek.ca/?p=175)). A character with an English Christian name, whose surname is itself a given name, fits the record. Reusing the surnames Isaac, Harper, Wood or Benjamin would tie the character to a real family whose descendants are active today.

**[TH]** Hän is critically endangered, and the nation is reviving it through classes, a working group and youth language leaders ([trondek.ca, Our Language](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-language/)). Elders quoted in different years count between two and fewer than fifteen fluent speakers ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.6600714); [YNLC](https://ynlc.ca/?p=2594), search summaries). **[Indigenous]** The Yukon Native Language Centre's Hän materials, built with Elders including Percy Henry, are licensed **CC BY-NC-SA (non-commercial)**, and using them in a project requires a written request ([YNLC, Hän](https://ynlc.ca/han/)). **[TH]** The Heritage Act reserves to the nation's Council the regulation of research with Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in people, of place names, and of communication protocols with outside parties (s. 20) ([Heritage Act](https://www.trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2016-08-28-Trondek-Hwechin-HERITAGE-ACT.pdf)). The place-name glossary in the nation's UNESCO support letter gives *Tr'ochëk* (the encampment at the Klondike mouth), *Tr'ondëk* (the Klondike River) and *Chu Kon'dëk* (the Yukon River). That glossary was seen only as a search summary ([UNESCO doc. 191129](https://whc.unesco.org/document/191129)).

**Moosehide's Hän name is unsettled.** The co-authored manual gives *Jëjik Dhä Dënezhu Kek'it* ([FNIC manual, p. 131](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)). Wikipedia gives *Dhä Dënezhu Kek'it* or *Ëdhä Dënezhu Kek'ìt*, with *Ëdhä Dädhëchan* as the Eagle Hän form ([Wikipedia, Moosehide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moosehide)). A 1994 oral history cited by TH carries a title that came through text extraction as *Moosehide (Édhä Dädhëchan Kek'èt)*, with the diacritics unreliable ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)). Use "Moosehide" in English, or ask TH staff for the current spelling.

## Songs, the dancing stick and the Moosehide Gathering are not the writer's to borrow

**[TH]** The nation tells the story of its songs as one of loss and return. When newcomers came "like a mosquito there, just by the thousand," Isaac sent the Hän songs, drums and the dancing stick to Alaskan relatives for safekeeping, and the nation is now relearning them from Alaskans (Elder Percy Henry, 1993) ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet, p. 1](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)). The DCB dates the handover itself to around 1912, so in 1898 the fear existed but the gift had not been made ([DCB](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/isaac_16E.html)). Potlatches did take place in this period. Isaac presided at the Eagle chief's funeral potlatch and at a 1904 Fortymile potlatch. The Moosehide Gathering, by contrast, is a modern revival, **first held in 1993** and biennial since, with 2024 its sixteenth ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/north/moosehide-gathering-trondek-hwechin-han-1.4767676); [trondek.ca](https://trondek.ca/moosehide-gathering), search summaries). It is an anachronism in 1898 and belongs to the nation. Moosehide village today is closed to visitors except during the Gathering ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moosehide)). The present-day Spring Camp in late April, held in 2025 at Nänkäk Chèholay ([trondek.ca](https://www.trondek.ca/event/trondek-hwechin-spring-camp/)), is also the nation's own modern practice, not 1898 color.

**No Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in protocol published specifically for fiction or games was found**, and no TH statement either welcomes or discourages outsiders using Hän words. The guidance that does exist all points the same way. **[TH]** The Heritage Act calls the nation's heritage "dynamic, living", names respect as "our biggest law", and gives Council authority over research and communication protocols ([Heritage Act](https://www.trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2016-08-28-Trondek-Hwechin-HERITAGE-ACT.pdf)). **[Indigenous]** imagineNATIVE's *On-Screen Protocols & Pathways* (2019) speaks directly to non-Indigenous creators. It tells them to start with the governing nation, and says that "all characters based partially or wholly on real people (living or dead) must be cleared", with the release for a deceased person coming from their representative. It calls for an Elders' advisory group, sensitivity reads to catch "inappropriate language use, and stereotypes", and payment for knowledge holders (its sample code suggests at least $200). Its default rule where permission is in doubt is "DO NOT film ceremonies." It warns against "poverty porn" and "traumatainment" ([On-Screen Protocols](https://www.efm-berlinale.de/media/pdf_word/efm/73_efm/on-screen-protocols-pathways.pdf)). The two best-known Indigenous game projects worked the same way. Never Alone was built with a partner studio chosen by the Cook Inlet Tribal Council, with Iñupiat Elders on the team and a story adapted with an Elder's permission ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/alaska-natives-share-their-stories-culture-through-commercial-games)). In When Rivers Were Trails, the Indigenous writers had the final say ([ICT News](https://ictnews.org/lifestyle/real-native-history-in-a-video-game-an-indigenous-take-on-the-oregon-trail/)). **[Outside]** Charlotte Gray names the patterns in gold-rush writing to avoid: an empty wilderness, Indigenous people left unnamed in the background, and their economic role erased ([Canada's History](https://www.canadashistory.ca/explore/peace-conflict/reconsidering-the-gold-rush)). A 1932 obituary reprinted by TH, praising Isaac's "friendliness to the whites," shows the condescending "friendly chief" voice a writer should not reproduce ([TH, Chief Isaac sheet](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)).

## Practical implications for writing the character

The record supports a specific, active, named person whose life in spring 1898 is busy with a move, a market and a grievance. It does not support a guide, a mystic or a victim. The table below turns the findings into rules. Items marked "inference" are design judgments drawn from the sources, not facts from them.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Give the character an English baptismal first name and an English surname, matching the 1898 record ([TH sheet](https://trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/chief_isaac_310303.pdf)) | Invent a Hän-language name, or use the surnames Isaac, Harper, Wood or Benjamin, or any full name of a documented person |
| Put them mid-move: building a cabin at the town's equivalent of Moosehide, about three miles downriver, while still using a tent (follows [trondek.ca](https://www.trondek.ca/our-story/our-territory/trochek/)) | Show a finished village, a church (St. Barnabas dates from 1908), a reserve (1900) or a Moosehide Gathering (1993) |
| Fill their April–May with documented work: the end of a late-winter or meat-sale hunt; grayling, waterfowl, muskrat and beaver; mending canoes and nets; tanning; sewing moccasins and gloves for sale ([TH Best Practices](https://www.trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/TH-Best-Practices-Heritage-Resources.pdf); [FNIC](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)) | Show salmon fishing or a salmon camp, or give a specific break-up date as fact |
| Make them a cash customer and seller at the trader's store: meat at famine prices, moccasins at $7, purchases of rifles, cloth and food (inference from [Gates](https://www.yukon-news.com/letters-opinions/chief-isaac-the-gentle-diplomat/)) | Invent a Hän credit or debt account as historical fact; it is undocumented |
| Let them carry the Tr'ochëk grievance: the people believed they had sold only the buildings, not the land, and the fish traps are gone ([FNIC](https://travolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/First-Nations-interpretative-manual.pdf)) | Make grievance their only trait, or use their suffering as spectacle ("traumatainment") |
| Write a Christian life alongside Hän practice, the "middle way" TH describes, with an Anglican missionary nearby (Flewelling, or Totty arriving in 1898) | Depict songs, drumming, the dancing stick, potlatch content, oral narratives or graves, or use them as clues |
| Have the inspector take their statement on affirmation or oath with an interpreter, and press them under the liquor-source clause, if the plot needs pressure (Indian Act structure; inference) | Present an 1898 legal rule on Indigenous testimony as settled fact beyond the 1876 text, or claim a documented Dawson precedent |
| If the town suspects them over the fire, label that as the town's prejudice, which the record supports, and let the evidence clear or decide it on its merits | Present a Hän person blamed for a Dawson fire as historical; the documented fires were blamed on saloon causes |
| Write dialogue in plain, fluent English with the character's own opinions (for example, that whites should leave hunting and fishing to the Hän, Isaac's documented theme) | Copy the newspapers' pidgin transcription of Isaac, or the "friendly to the whites" voice of the 1932 obituary |
| Keep Chief Isaac offstage or limited to documented actions (negotiating the move, sponsoring the meat hunt) | Give Isaac invented lines, motives or a role in the crime; real people need clearance |
| Use English place names, plus *Tr'ochëk* and *Tr'ondëk* if wanted, and show "Lousetown/Klondike City" alongside them | Use Hän vocabulary from YNLC or other material (non-commercial licence), or an unverified Moosehide name |
| Contact the TH Heritage department before writing, and pay and credit a Hän sensitivity reader with final say over the character ([Heritage Act](https://www.trondek.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2016-08-28-Trondek-Hwechin-HERITAGE-ACT.pdf); [On-Screen Protocols](https://www.efm-berlinale.de/media/pdf_word/efm/73_efm/on-screen-protocols-pathways.pdf)) | Treat this research as a substitute for the nation's review; a Heritage department email address appeared only in a search summary and is unverified ([Arctic article](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/67289/51199/189651)) |

## Conclusion

The sources together suggest a stronger premise than "a Hän witness in a mining town". In April–May 1898 the Hän had their own unresolved case about property. Buildings were sold and land was taken, the buyers and sellers understood the deal differently, and a petition sat for a year in Ottawa while the police staked the ground. A mystery about a burned store, questioned by a Mounted Police inspector, can let that history shape how the character reads the investigation: as one more outside authority deciding whose word and whose ownership count. The weakest point in the record is the one the game depends on most. No 1896–98 source shows how a Hän witness's word was actually weighed, so the game's credibility mechanic will be a design choice, not a reconstruction, and it should say so.

The second takeaway is about process. Every Indigenous-authored source in this research, including the TH Heritage Act, imagineNATIVE's protocols, the YNLC licence and the Never Alone model, treats permission as part of getting the facts right, not as a formality. The disputed Moosehide name, the uncertain move date and the unread *Hammerstones* are exactly the gaps the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in Heritage department can close. Asking them is the most reliable way to get those details right, as well as the respectful one.
